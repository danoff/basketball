Which 2026 rookies will actually defend?

A five-variable linear model trained on three rookie classes (2023–2025) uses pre-draft weight, hand width, standing vertical, lane agility, and wingspan to project rookie-year Defensive BPM — restricted to rookies who actually saw the floor for at least 100 NBA minutes. Meant as a scouting prompt, not a verdict.

2Reading the coefficients

Two takeaways · 60 seconds
Reading I · Mass over length
A 10-pound increase in weight lines up with about +0.22 DBPM — the strongest signal in the model (p = 0.003). After three years of rookie data, functional physicality — the body that can survive NBA contact — beats raw length.
Reading II · Hand width is real
Each extra inch of hand width is worth roughly +0.39 DBPM (p = 0.037). Wider hands plausibly translate to deflections, contests, and rebound control — ball-disruption defense, not rim protection.

5What this isn't

Read before quoting
This is an exploratory model trained on 89 rookies across three draft classes (2023–2025). DBPM is a noisy outcome shaped by team context, role, scheme, and minutes. The model explains roughly a quarter of the variance in rookie DBPM. Treat individual numbers as a conversation starter, never a final grade.
Sample size
89 rookies passed the 100-minute filter across three classes — useful but still small for inference.
Significance
Weight (p = 0.003) and hand width (p = 0.037) are significant. Wingspan's negative sign is significant but most likely a multicollinearity artefact.
Missing inputs
Skill, IQ, motor, scheme, role — all of it lives outside this model. Position labels are intentionally excluded.
Survivorship
Training is restricted to rookies with MP ≥ 100. Washouts are out; the bar will bend upward.

Method
Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression in Python, trained on 2023–2025 draft classes restricted to rookies with at least 100 NBA minutes (n = 89). Five predictors, intercept fit. Code, data, and notebooks open-sourced on GitHub.
